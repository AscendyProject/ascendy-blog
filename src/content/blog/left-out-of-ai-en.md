## TL;DR

- I pay a few hundred dollars a month in subscriptions without much strain, and I'm inside a virtuous cycle of [using that as time leverage](/en/blog/you-get-5-hours-a-day-en/). But it hit me — *the people on the other side of that.*
- **Some people won't use AI even when it's free.** The barrier isn't only money — it's having no *occasion,* no *will* to use it. AI is powerful leverage *when you have productive work to do;* with no such work, it goes unused even handed to you free.
- This isn't one person's story. Korea's **"resting youth"** — those neither working nor job-hunting — passed **460,000,** a five-year high. And I've been there too: I **failed an exam and spent about half a year out of work.** In that state, I didn't even want to do anything productive.
- So AI may accelerate the gap along the axis of *will and occasion,* not money. You can subsidize access; you can't subsidize drive. I don't have a clean answer. But what pulled *me* out wasn't a *cheaper tool* — it was **a spark and an on-ramp.**

> **About this piece.** This isn't a piece that pretends to know the answer — it's closer to an honest question I'm putting out while unsure of the fix. After writing [AI is leverage](/en/blog/you-get-5-hours-a-day-en/), this is a kind of conscience about the people who *can't grab* that leverage. The friend here is generalized so as not to be identifiable.

## A friend who won't use it even for free

Talking with a friend who's currently out of work, AI came up. They said they **barely use it.** No work occasion to use it, don't feel much need, and grudge the subscription cost — so they use the free tier now and then, search-style.

Of course I didn't brag *"here's how much I use it."* I just listened quietly, and one thought landed. **The gap between me — employed, using AI at work, running it on my own dime — and this friend could widen even more in ten years.**

Here's what I almost missed. At first I thought "so it's a money problem." It wasn't. They can actually **use AI for free** — the free tier exists. And they still don't. Because — *they have no reason to.*

## The barrier isn't money — it's a reason

This is the crux. **AI is an enormously powerful tool *when you're doing something productive.*** Writing code, drafting documents, running analysis — it's leverage *when there's work to do.*

Flip it, and *with no work to do,* that power has nowhere to land. For someone out of work with no task to apply it to — and therefore no felt need — AI goes largely unused however cheap, even free. **The barrier isn't $20; it's whether you have a *reason* to use AI at all.**

So the gap splits somewhere deeper than "those who can pay the subscription vs. those who can't." **Compounding accrues only to those with the occasion and will to use AI;** for others, even free access doesn't open a shut door.

## This isn't one person's story

If it were just my one friend, it'd end as a personal anecdote. It doesn't.

Korea's **"resting youth"** — young people who report neither working nor job-hunting, just resting — hit [469,000 in January 2026, a five-year high](https://biz.heraldcorp.com/article/10674465). And the Bank of Korea, in one issue note, points to **"AI-driven technological change and a preference for experienced hires"** as among the structural factors worsening the youth labor market.

So AI works doubly here. On one side it shrinks the entry-level slots juniors would take (pushing youth out of the labor market); on the other it grows the productivity of those already working, as leverage. **The distance between the pushed-out and the riding-on is, in effect, widened by AI from both sides.**

## I've been there too

I don't want to write this as someone else's problem. Because **I've been inside it.**

I studied for a high-stakes exam and failed, and then spent about half a year out of work. Looking back — the very *desire* to do something productive barely arose. However powerful a tool sat in front of me, free or paid, I had no *reason to pick it up.* Having been in that state, I know: **how hollow "AI's great, just try it" sounds to someone inside it.**

So I won't split this gap into "lazy vs. diligent." That's a story about a *state,* not about a person's worth.

## So what can be done — what pulled me out

So the real question is this. *If access (free AI) isn't the answer, what pulls a person out of that state?* For me, there were two things.

**First, a spark.** Back when I was studying for that exam, I watched [the 2016 match between AlphaGo and Lee Sedol](/en/blog/have-you-even-tried-it-en/). Just watching, the thought came: *"building something like that would be fun."* Not a grand resolution — one small flicker of interest.

**Second, an on-ramp.** After failing and drifting as an out-of-work young man, while looking into "maybe I'll become a developer," I found the SC IT Master program at the Korea International Trade Association's academy — a 20-plus-year government-funded course geared to IT employment in Japan (its graduates number in the thousands). **A complete computer illiterate, I switched careers to developer there and even landed a job in Japan.**

Here's the point. What pulled me out wasn't a *cheaper AI.* It was **a reason to pick it up (the spark) and a structured path that carries a beginner to an actual job (the on-ramp).** And only once that job existed did I get an *occasion* to use AI at all. The order is reversed — not the tool first, but the reason first.

## Honestly — I don't have the answer

That doesn't mean I know the fix. Can sparks be created? Can on-ramps be widened? Probably, somewhat. But **you cannot hand someone the will itself.** Scatter the tool for free, and those without the drive to try something won't use it much. I was one of them.

So this piece closes on a question, not an answer. If AI is tilting toward *accelerating* the gap, what we can touch is probably *upstream* of access — **the spark and the on-ramp.** Making people a *reason* to use it. That's harder than handing out cheap tools, which is exactly why I can't offer a tidy answer.

One thing is clear, though. I'm standing on the side that holds this leverage, and on the other side are people like I once was. Pretending not to see that, and writing only "AI is the best tool," felt dishonest.

## Takeaways

- **People won't use it even for free.** The barrier to the AI gap isn't only money — it's whether you have the *occasion and will* to use AI.
- **AI can widen the gap from both sides.** It shrinks entry-level slots and pushes people out, while growing working people's productivity as leverage.
- **I've been there.** Out of work, I had no reason to pick up even a powerful tool. This is about a *state,* not a person's worth.
- **The reason comes before the access.** What pulled me out wasn't cheaper AI but a spark (interest) and an on-ramp (a structured path). You can't hand over drive, but you can make sparks and on-ramps.

Honestly this is a record of discomfort, not a solution. But if I'm going to write about using AI as time leverage, honesty means also writing about the people who can't grab that leverage.
