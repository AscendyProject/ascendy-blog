- I became a developer because of 2016, [when AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol](/en/blog/have-you-even-tried-it-en/). Watching it, I thought *"building something like that would be fun."* But the mood in the world was one thing — **"humans can't beat AI at Go anymore."**

## I got into coding when AlphaGo beat Lee Sedol

A confession first. I wasn't originally a developer. What pulled me onto this path was 2016, the **AlphaGo vs Lee Sedol match.** Just watching, I thought *"building something like that would be fun,"* and that was the start.

But the message the world read in that match was much heavier. *Go* had been something like the last sanctuary of human intuition, and it fell. **"Humans can no longer beat AI at Go."** Many took it that way, and after that the narrative "as AI advances, humans go obsolete" spread across field after field.

## Then this week, the opposite scene arrived

In July 2026, [world number one Shin Jinseo won a two-stone handicap game against KataGo, the strongest Go AI in existence](https://www.hankyung.com/article/202607192656i). A close fight to move 290, won by 4.5 points.

To be honest, this isn't a clean-sweep story. **Over a two-day match he lost the first game outright and won the second — 1–1.** Even so, what makes the win special is *which* AI it was against. **KataGo is the strongest Go AI that exists today, built on the very AlphaGo that toppled Lee Sedol a decade ago.** Its algorithms and training are beyond comparison with back then. Against that, a human laid down two stones and won.

People who know Go rate this win as *comparable to Lee Sedol's single game.* (With engines so much stronger now, exactly what level "Shin taking two stones from KataGo" meets "the AlphaGo of back then" at is hard to measure precisely — that's the realm of estimate. But the direction is clear.)

## Not "caught up" — the lens changed, and humans evolved

So some people say: "See, humans caught up to AI in the end." I think that phrasing is wrong. *Catching up* is a picture of someone behind chasing someone ahead on the same track — but that's not what happened here.

Over the past decade, pro players studied Go with AI as a *teacher and sparring partner.* And in doing so, **the very way they see Go changed.** Moves believed to be settled were overturned; moves humans never played became standard. Shin Jinseo's Go isn't *a good preservation of the human Go of the past.* It's a **new Go, evolved by passing through the lens of AI.**

That is, humans didn't chase AI down and catch it. **Inside the new field of view that AI threw open, humans re-evolved themselves.** AI evolved, so humans evolved too.

## The same for developers — from horses to cars

Here it comes back to my own story. I think this shape applies directly to the **developer in the age of AI.**

Honestly, the place of the skill I hone has moved. I used to care about being good at debugging, at knowing a language's syntax precisely. Now — *I barely even think about building those abilities.* Some developers will call this **degeneration.** But I call it **evolution.**

Here's the analogy. **Even after the car was invented,** some people still study horsemanship deeply, and that has its own value. But the world's center of gravity shifted toward *driving skill* and *automotive engineering.* What advanced wasn't riding a horse better, but building and driving a car better.

The developer in the age of AI is like **someone who used to ride a horse and hone horsemanship becoming someone who drives a car and hones driving.** The place you hone moved from syntax and debugging (horsemanship) to setting direction and judging results (driving). And at the pinnacle is the **F1 driver** — someone who knows the automotive engineering *and* drives the best. A seat that masters both the machine and its operation, to the extreme.

## So can you know nothing? — a captain doesn't hold the helm

Here I have to draw a line. "So you delegate everything to AI and a developer can know nothing?" — no. An evolved developer and someone *just carried along* by AI are different.

I draw the line this way. **On a ship, the captain doesn't hold the helm.** A big ship has a helmsman and professional navigators. And yet we say *the captain steers the ship.* Because the captain **makes the judgments that get the ship safely to its destination, and bears responsibility for that voyage.**

What separates a driver from a passenger isn't *whether your hands touch the controls.* It's **whether you're making the judgments and holding the destination.** A developer who hands implementation to AI but keeps direction and judgment is a *captain* who doesn't hold the helm — not a *passenger* being carried. Conversely, drifting along with whatever AI spits out, with no judgment, is being a passenger no matter how much output appears.

## So — "AI's rise makes humans obsolete" may be a wrong prediction

Ten years ago, before AlphaGo, what we felt was the fear of obsolescence. But Shin Jinseo's two stones show that prediction is *at least not that simple.*

Here's what I want to say. **Even to a new tool like AI — and to its almost-impossible-to-adapt-to, staggering rate of advancement — humans are adapting again.** As in Go, so in development. When the tool evolves, humans aren't pushed aside; they *re-evolve themselves inside the new field of view the tool opened.*

Of course this is my bet. Whether to choose obsolescence or evolution is, in the end, each person's own. But as someone who started down this path ten years ago *because* I felt "humans lost" — I rather liked this week's two stones.

## Takeaways

- **AI evolves. Humans evolve too.** Not by preserving the same skill, but by *relocating the one they hone.*
- **Not "caught up" but "the lens changed, and they evolved."** Shin Jinseo's Go is a new Go passed through the lens of AI. Development is the same.
- **From horses to cars.** The skill domain moved from syntax and debugging to direction and judgment. Not decay but evolution. The pinnacle is the F1 driver who knows both the machine and the operation.
- **A captain steers without holding the helm.** What separates a driver from a passenger is judgment and destination, not whether a hand touches the controls.

The narrative that "as AI advances, humans go obsolete" may be a prediction we believed too quickly.
